Do High Schools Shape College Choices?
Evidence from Centralized Assignment in
                Chile
                       Bruno E. Aravena-Magüida

                              September 2026


                                   Abstract

     High schools may shape students’ postsecondary choices in ways that are not
captured by test-score measures of school quality. I study this question using
Chile’s centralized school assignment system, where random tie-breakers generate
lottery variation in high-school offers. I link these lotteries to administrative
data on achievement, higher-education applications, and enrollment, and construct
observational school value-added measures for seven outcomes spanning achievement,
postsecondary access, and the institution and field students ultimately choose. I
first ask whether these observational measures predict causal school effects. They
do. Lottery-induced attendance at schools with higher outcome-specific value added
improves the corresponding outcome for all seven measures. Forecast coefficients
are close to one for math and verbal achievement and range from 0.49 to 0.86
for postsecondary access and higher-education choices. School effectiveness is
also strongly multidimensional. After removing correlated sampling noise, math
and verbal value added are highly correlated, while value added for attending
high-premium institutions and high-premium fields is only weakly related. These
cross-outcome relationships also have causal content: the gains implied by the
covariance structure broadly line up with direct lottery estimates. High schools
therefore shape not only whether students reach higher education, but also the
options they ultimately choose within it. Choices that appear to reflect student
preferences may partly reflect the high school attended.

1     Introduction
School quality is usually summarized by achievement. This is natural: test scores are
widely available, comparable across schools, and predictive of later outcomes (Chetty
et al., 2011). But school and teacher effects need not be one-dimensional. Recent work
shows that schools and teachers affect attendance, behavior, crime, teenage motherhood,
labor-market outcomes, and other long-run outcomes in ways that are only weakly related
to their test-score effects (Jackson, 2018; Beuermann et al., 2023; Rose et al., 2022;
Angrist et al., 2023). If families and policymakers care about these other dimensions,
achievement-based measures of school quality may be seriously incomplete.
    This broader view of school quality is especially relevant for high schools. High
schools are the last formal educational institutions students attend before making higher-
education choices, and those choices are highly consequential. Whether students enter
higher education, which institutions they attend, and which fields they choose all matter
because returns vary substantially across institutions and, especially, across fields of study
(Kirkeboen et al., 2016). Yet measures of school effects on higher education often focus on
relatively coarse outcomes such as enrollment or persistence rather than on the type of
institution or field students enter.
    There are good reasons to expect high schools to shape these choices. Prior work
documents large differences in application and enrollment behavior across groups of
students and shows that relatively small interventions can change college search, application
behavior, and major choice (Hoxby and Turner, 2015; Dynarski et al., 2021; Hastings
et al., 2016; Chetty et al., 2025). High schools may differ in counseling, information
provision, expectations, peer norms, academic confidence, or preparation for particular
fields. If so, their effects on higher education may extend well beyond their effects on
achievement.
    This possibility also changes how higher-education choices should be interpreted.
Decisions about whether to attend higher education, which institution to attend, or which
field to study are often treated as revealing students’ preferences given the options they
face. But the information students have, the options they perceive, and their preparation
for different fields may themselves depend on the high school they attend. A choice
can therefore be privately optimal given beliefs and preparation that the school helped
produce, while still being causally affected by the school attended.
    This paper asks two questions. First, do high schools causally affect postsecondary
access and higher-education choices, and not only achievement? More precisely, do
outcome-specific observational value-added measures predict causal school effects on these
outcomes, using a validation approach so far applied primarily to test scores (Angrist
et al., 2017)? Second, do these effects reflect a single underlying dimension of school
quality, or do schools have distinct strengths across outcomes?
    The main empirical challenge is that students and families sort across schools. Families
choose schools based on resources, preferences, networks, location, and expectations,
and these same characteristics may shape later higher-education choices. Differences

in outcomes across schools may therefore reflect both causal school effects and the
characteristics of the students who select into them.
    I address this challenge using Chile’s centralized school assignment system, the Sistema
de Admisión Escolar (SAE). SAE assigns students to public and private-subsidized schools,
which serve over 90 percent of high-school students, using a deferred-acceptance mechanism
with random tie-breakers when schools are oversubscribed. I replicate the assignment
mechanism and use its lottery variation to isolate changes in the high school students
attend. I link these assignments to national administrative data on school enrollment,
standardized achievement, higher-education applications, financial-aid applications, and
enrollment.
    I first estimate observational school value added for seven outcomes in a broad national
panel of roughly 758,000 students. The outcomes span achievement, postsecondary access,
and choices within higher education: math and verbal achievement, admission-exam
taking, financial-aid applications, higher-education enrollment, and enrollment in high-
premium institutions and fields. The value-added measures are school fixed effects from
regressions with rich pre-treatment controls, including grade-4 standardized test scores,
family background, and middle-school performance, and I use Empirical Bayes to reduce
sampling noise. I then ask whether the schools that look better observationally on a given
outcome also causally improve that outcome. For roughly 136,000 SAE applicants whose
assignments depend on random tie-breakers, I use the value added of the school offered
by the mechanism as an instrument for the value added of the school ultimately attended,
accounting for the assignment risk implied by each student’s submitted preferences and
priorities.
    Using the terminology of Angrist et al. (2017), I summarize this relationship with a
forecast coefficient, ψ.1 If ψ = 1, the causal gain induced by the lottery matches the gain
predicted by observational value added for that outcome. If ψ = 0, the observational
measure has no causal predictive content along the lottery-induced margin.
    Outcome-specific observational value added contains substantial causal signal across
all seven margins. For achievement, the forecast coefficients are 1.20 for math and 1.03
for verbal achievement, close to a one-for-one relationship between observational value
added and lottery-induced gains. The corresponding coefficients are 0.49 for admission-
exam taking, 0.58 for financial-aid applications, and 0.74 for higher-education enrollment.
Schools that observationally increase enrollment in high-premium institutions and fields
also causally shift students toward those choices, with forecast coefficients of 0.67 and 0.86.
Thus, observational school value added predicts causal effects not only on achievement or
whether students enter the postsecondary system, but also on the types of options they
ultimately choose within it.
    I next ask whether these school effects are different manifestations of a common
dimension of school quality. To separate genuine covariance in school effectiveness from
correlated sampling noise, I adapt the cross-cohort estimator of Rose et al. (2022) to
schools. The resulting covariance structure is far from one-dimensional. Math and

     I then test whether these observational relationships across outcomes have causal
content. For each pair of outcomes, I compare the cross-outcome gain implied by the
value-added covariance structure with a direct lottery-based estimate. Across the 42
directed outcome pairs, the implied and direct causal gains have a correlation of 0.45 and
agree in sign in 79 percent of cases. The agreement is not exact, but it shows that the
multidimensional structure observed across schools contains meaningful information about
how lottery-induced changes in school attendance affect outcomes beyond the one used to
rank schools.
     Descriptive evidence on school characteristics reinforces this picture. Administration
type, school size, and curricular track explain about 40 percent of the variation in math and
verbal value added, but only about 2 percent of the variation in high-premium-field value
added. Staffing patterns and qualifications are also associated with different dimensions
in different ways: teacher staffing and credentials are most systematically related to
achievement, while detailed staff characteristics add substantial explanatory power for
high-premium-institution value added. Field-choice value added remains comparatively
difficult to predict from the observable school inputs available here. These relationships are
not causal, but they suggest that the school features associated with raising achievement
need not be the same as those associated with shaping later educational choices.
     The paper contributes to three literatures. First, it contributes to work on teacher and
school value added. A large literature estimates the effects of teachers and schools on test
scores and longer-run outcomes (Chetty et al., 2014; Dobbie and Fryer, 2015; Jackson,
2018; Beuermann et al., 2023; Rose et al., 2022). This work increasingly emphasizes
that test-score value added may miss important dimensions of educational production. I
show that this multidimensionality extends to consequential postsecondary outcomes at
the high-school stage: schools differ not only in their effects on achievement and overall
enrollment, but also in their effects on whether students enter high-premium institutions
and fields. I causally validate these outcome-specific measures and then use their cross-
outcome covariance to characterize which dimensions of school effectiveness move together.
Methodologically, I adapt the cross-cohort covariance estimator of Rose et al. (2022) to
schools and compare the cross-outcome gains it implies with direct lottery-based causal
estimates.
     Second, the paper contributes to the literature that uses lotteries and centralized
assignment to estimate school effects (Abdulkadiroğlu et al., 2014; Abdulkadiroğlu et al.,
2017; Angrist et al., 2024). In the spirit of Angrist et al. (2017), I ask whether observational
value added is a useful guide to causal school effects. I apply this logic to a nationwide

    Third, the paper contributes to work on higher-education choice. Prior research
shows that the institution and field students choose matter for later outcomes, and that
information and application frictions can change those choices (Kirkeboen et al., 2016;

system itself generates causal differences in students’ access to and choices within higher
education. The results imply that choices which appear to reveal student preferences may
partly reflect the high school attended.

2       Context and Data
2.1     Institutional Setting
Chile is a useful setting for this project because national administrative data records
school enrollment, academic histories, standardized achievement, and higher-education
choices with individual identifiers that allow students to be matched across educational
data.
   Formal secondary education begins in grade 9 and lasts four years. Students may
attend public, private-subsidized, or private non-subsidized schools. Public and private-
subsidized schools are fully or partly financed by the State and thus participate in the
centralized school assignment system described below. They serve 90.6% of the high-school
population. Private non-subsidized schools remain outside that mechanism and serve the
remaining 9.4%.


2.2     Centralized School Assignment
Chile’s centralized school assignment system, the Sistema de Admisión Escolar (SAE),
assigns students to participating schools through a version of student-proposing Deferred
Acceptance. Families submit ranked lists of schools. The mechanism combines these
rankings with school vacancies and priority rules, and it uses random tie-breakers when
there are more applicants than seats within a priority group. If a student’s offered
school depends on one of these tie-breakers in the system, different lottery draws will
imply different school assignments. I refer to such students as being at assignment risk.
Conditional on the information that determines priorities and assignment probabilities,
the random tie-breakers generate quasi-experimental variation in school assignment, as
described by Abdulkadiroğlu et al. (2017).
    SAE started in 2017 in some regions and was rolled out nationally in 2018. Given that
I study high-school effects, I use applications to grade-9 vacancies from the 2018–2020
grade-8 cohorts. These cohorts are expected to finish high school between 2022 and 2024,
allowing me to observe high-school attendance and initial postsecondary outcomes.
    Implementing this design requires recovering each applicant’s probability of assignment
to each school among those in the submitted rankings of schools. I do this by replicating
the SAE assignment algorithm and repeatedly simulating counterfactual lottery draws,
which determines which students are at assignment risk. As a diagnostic check, when I
run the replicated algorithm using the original lottery numbers for the 2018–2020 regular
grade-9 processes, I reproduce over 99% of observed first-round assignments in each of the
three cohorts. 2 The remaining mismatch reflects institutional features that are difficult
to fully recover from public documentation, but the high replication rate gives confidence
that the simulated probabilities capture the relevant assignment risk.

    For this exercise and the rest of the paper, I define high-school attendance as the school
where a student spends the most time after grade 8, with most recent spells breaking
ties. This definition smooths over short enrollment spells and transfers, and it assigns
a single high-school value to each student. As a result, students who did not receive a
first-round offer might still end up attending a certain high-school via complementary
rounds or an application cycle in a later year. On average, a high school is attended by
6.9% of students rejected from it in the first round and by 62.5% of students offered a
seat. The resulting difference of approximately 55.6 percentage points establishes a strong
first stage for school attendance.


2.3     Sample Description
This subsection describes the two samples used in the analysis. The first is the value-
added sample, a broad student panel used to estimate outcome-specific school fixed
effects after adjusting for a rich set of covariates. The second is the lottery sample,

which contains students whose timely grade-9 SAE applications generate nondegenerate
assignment probabilities. I use this second sample to test whether lottery-induced changes
in attended-school value added produce the outcome gains predicted by the observational
measures.

2.3.1   Value Added Sample

I construct the value added sample by merging annual enrollment, grade progression,
attendance, grades, and completion records using the student identifier provided for
educational data. The linked administrative panel starts from the universe of students
observed in grade 8 between 2017 and 2020, producing 944,510 students across four cohorts
defined by their year of grade-8 enrollment. For instance, a student in the 2019 cohort
who wants to change schools within the non-private system applies to SAE that year, is
expected to start high school in 2020, and is expected to graduate in 2023.
    To construct school value added, I retain as much of this broad panel as possible
while imposing the restrictions required for comparable high-school exposure and baseline
controls. From this universe I drop students younger than 12 or older than 16 in grade 8
and those who do not attend high school.
    Requiring a complete vector of baseline covariates is the most consequential sample
restriction. For these covariates, I use information from Chile’s national standardized
assessment system (SIMCE) and middle school relative performance. Middle school
controls are constructed by comparing attendance and GPA of a student to those of their
peers in middle school every year. These are then averaged and summarized into two
middle school z-scores for a student: one for attendance and one for GPA.
    SIMCE exams are administered in different grades depending on the year. This always
includes grade 4 and occasionally grades 8 and/or 10. Unfortunately grade-8 and grade-10
measures are not available for all the studied cohorts. I therefore use grade-4 SIMCE as
the main pre-treatment achievement control.
    SIMCE also collects parent surveys. I use these surveys to measure family background
before treatment, including household income, parental education, indigenous origin of the
parents, and early-childhood education. These variables are useful because they proxy for
family resources and prior investments that may influence both high-school choices and
later higher-education outcomes. When parent-survey variables are missing, I use middle
school and neighborhood cells to impute medians where enough donor observations are
available; remaining missing values are left missing.

For the lottery sample, I follow the same initial steps using the 2018–2020 cohorts, the
first cohorts for which SAE operated nationally. Panel B of Table 1 repeats the accounting

2.5     Higher Education Outcomes
The seven primary outcomes are organized into the same three groups used throughout
the results tables: achievement, postsecondary access, and within higher-education choice.
    The achievement group contains math and verbal scores from the national higher-
education admission exam. Taking this exam is itself a choice, but it is the standard
route into selective higher education. Applicants take mandatory math and verbal
components and may also take optional subject exams.4 I standardize math and verbal
scores within test year to make them comparable across test regimes; these two outcomes
are consequently observed only for exam takers.
    The postsecondary-access group contains three binary outcomes defined for the broader
eligible student population. The first is admission-exam taking. The second is whether
the student submitted any application through the centralized state system for higher-
education scholarships and student credit (FUAS). The third is whether the student
appears in the higher-education enrollment records. Students who do not take the exam,
submit a financial-aid application, or enroll are coded zero for the respective outcome.
    The final group describes within-higher-education choices: whether a student enrolls
in a high-premium institution and whether a student enrolls in a high-premium field.
Higher-education applications and admissions are organized at the program–institution
level, so the enrollment records identify both the institution and field associated with a
student’s first observed enrollment.
    Students in the sample have not yet finished higher education, so I cannot observe
their realized earnings. I instead use MiFuturo, an information source maintained by
the Ministry of Education that reports program-level labor-market statistics, including
average income in the fourth year after graduation. Because MiFuturo identifiers do
not map one-to-one into every program code in the enrollment records, I estimate a
log-income model with institution and generic-program fixed effects. These estimates
provide a common measure of the earnings premium associated with each institution and
generic program. Figure 2 shows the generic-program fixed effects organized by field.
    The high-premium-field indicator equals one when the student’s first observed enroll-
ment is in Science, Law, Engineering/Manufacturing/Construction, or Medicine+.5 All
other observed fields, including programs outside the retained field taxonomy, are coded
zero. The high-premium-institution indicator equals one when the institution associated
with the student’s first observed enrollment has a centered MiFuturo institution fixed
effect above 0.1 log points.6

these two outcomes: an institution or field can be classified only when an enrollment choice
is observed. The outcomes used in the main regressions are nevertheless unconditional with

3       Empirical Framework
This section describes the empirical objects and research design. The goal is not to
estimate a separate causal effect for every school and every possible margin of substitution.
Instead, I ask whether outcome-specific observational measures of school value added
predict causal effects generated by lottery-induced changes in high-school attendance. The
design therefore summarizes school effectiveness with scalar outcome-specific indices and
estimates a forecast coefficient for each outcome.

4.3             Correlates of Value Added
The preceding results show that schools differ substantially across several dimensions
of value added. A natural next question is whether these differences are systematically
related to observable school characteristics. The lottery design does not identify the
causal effects of these characteristics: lotteries shift the school a student attends, but do
not independently randomize school inputs such as staffing, size, or administration type.
Everything in this section is therefore descriptive. A correlation between value added and,
for example, the age composition of the teaching staff may reflect the effect of that input,
the characteristics of schools able to attract such staff, or other school features correlated
with both. The goal is to describe where each dimension of school quality is concentrated
and which dimensions are well summarized by observable school characteristics.

4.3.1             Administration type, size, and curricular track

Table 6 regresses each EB value-added measure on indicators for administration type,
school size, and curricular track. Value added is expressed in the units of each outcome
and centered at the student-weighted mean, so coefficients measure differences relative to

Achievement, postsecondary access, institution choice, and field choice are related but
distinct dimensions of school effectiveness. Even two outcomes that might appear closely
connected—placing students in high-premium institutions and placing them in high-
premium fields—are only weakly related across schools.
    This matters for how school quality is interpreted. A ranking based on achievement
value added contains some information about schools’ effects on later outcomes, but it
is far from a sufficient summary. Schools that look similar when ranked by achievement
can have very different effects on whether students enter higher education and on the
institutions and fields they ultimately choose.

5     Conclusion
High schools shape more than students’ academic achievement. Using lottery variation
from Chile’s centralized school assignment system, I show that observational measures of
school value added predict causal effects on postsecondary access and higher-education
choices as well as on achievement. At the same time, these dimensions of school effectiveness
are far from interchangeable. Schools that improve achievement are not necessarily those
that move students toward higher education, and schools that increase enrollment in
high-premium institutions are only weakly related to those that increase enrollment in
high-premium fields.
    This multidimensionality changes how school effects on later choices should be inter-
preted. Decisions about whether to enter higher education, which institution to attend,
and which field to study are ultimately made by students. But the information, prepa-
ration, expectations, and opportunities underlying those decisions may themselves be
shaped by the high school attended. Postsecondary choices therefore need not simply
reveal fixed student preferences; they can also be outcomes of the educational environment
that precedes them.
    These results also have implications for how school quality is summarized and com-
municated. School rankings and school-choice information are often organized around
achievement, implicitly treating test-score performance as a sufficient statistic for school
quality. The results here suggest that this can be misleading. Achievement value added is
informative, but it is only imperfectly related to schools’ effects on postsecondary access
and on the institutions and fields students ultimately choose. For families who care about
these later outcomes, an achievement-based ranking can therefore give an incomplete
picture of the consequences of attending one school rather than another. More generally,
there may be no single ranking of high schools that is appropriate for all outcomes students
and families care about.
    Understanding why schools differ across these dimensions is an important next step.
The associations with school organization and staff characteristics documented here
provide some initial clues, but they do not identify the underlying mechanisms. The
cohorts studied in this paper are also still early in their postsecondary and labor-market
trajectories. As additional outcomes become observable, it will be possible to ask whether
the school-induced differences in institution and field choice documented here translate
into differences in degree completion, earnings, and other longer-run outcomes. More
broadly, understanding what schools produce requires looking not only at what students
know when they leave school, but also at the choices they make afterward.
